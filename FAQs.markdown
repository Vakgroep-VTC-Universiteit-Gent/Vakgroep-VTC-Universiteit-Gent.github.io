### Access to GenAI tools

#### Does Ghent University provide free access to GenAI tools?

Thanks to an agreement with Microsoft, Ghent University can offer its members free access to the **Microsoft Copilot** tool. Note, however, that the functionalities offered by the Ghent University version of Copilot are limited compared to the standard version. When developing a custom chatbot ("Agent"), for example, it is not possible to add SharePoint files as knowledge. What the agreement does include is better data protection than you typically get with other commercial GenAI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Le Chat. Theoretically, the data you upload into Copilot cannot be used by Microsoft to train their AI models. However, this by no means implies that you can safely enter any kind of data into Copilot: sensitive and/or personal data should still always be properly anonymised or pseudonymised before uploaded to the Copilot environment. More information on accessing Copilot with your Ghent University account can be found at the following (Dutch) web page: [https://www.ugent.be/nl/univgent/missie/genai/copilot-en-andere-tools](https://www.ugent.be/nl/univgent/missie/genai/copilot-en-andere-tools).

### Capabilities GenAI tools

#### Are GenAI tools capable of writing a summary of a spoken text?

Yes, tools such as [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google/) (from Google) can perform this task reasonably to very well. Additionally, it is also possible to turn the task into a two-step process: first you use a tool that transcribes the spoken text into written text (e.g., [WhisperAI](https://whisperai.com/)) and secondly you use a tool that generates the summary based on the transcription (which can be done with any GenAI tool, basically). As with many tasks, the result will be best when the input language is English, but also for other languages the results are often more than decent.

### Prompting

#### How can I teach my students to write good prompts?

Developing a prompt which results in an output message that exactly corresponds to what you had in mind (a process often referred to as "prompt engineering") can be a difficult task for students, especially if they are not really familiar with using GenAI tools and/or if they are not yet real experts in the subject matter the prompt is about. Therefore, as a teacher, it might be worth considering to provide a "prompt template" to your students. Through this template, you provide your students with the "framework" in which they can simply just "plug in" a specific question or piece of content. The underlying idea is that, by providing that template, you "steer" your students into a direction that will very likely lead to useful generated output. Designing a good template takes some time and comes with trial and error, but the "return on investment" should be high enough if your students can use your template for a variety of exercises. One thing you should definitely consider when designing your template is integrating a few well-chosen examples of what the output should look like (a technique called **few-shot prompting**). As GenAI models are particularly good at recognising and mimicking patterns, this approach will very likely lead to better results compared to simply asking your question without adding examples (i.e. zero-shot prompting).
